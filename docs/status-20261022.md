# Status (2026-10-22)

Motion-model numbers are LVE/MHD in mm and FDD on the 485-clip held-out split, scored with the same code and clips as the 2026-10-13 status (head pose not scored). Renderer numbers are LPIPS (AlexNet) and PSNR against GAGAvatar's own output. Conversation numbers are seconds from the end of the user's voiced speech to the first voiced reply sample at the app's bridge, on one A100. Every number below was re-derived from the raw outputs by a separate check.

## 1. Results for the directions agreed on 2026-10-13

**Browser renderer: real trained weights driven by real streamed motion hold 25 fps for 10 minutes on a fanless MacBook Air M4. Retraining on 887 identities removed most of the overfitting, but the network still misses the quality bar on new faces.**

- Retraining on 887 faces (885 tracked from the motion corpus's own videos plus the bundled examples; 48 new faces held out, plus the two held-out portraits from the previous run):

| against GAGAvatar | 512 px network | 256 px-core network | bar |
|---|---|---|---|
| 48 new faces (video frames) | LPIPS 0.044, 28.3 dB | 0.049, 29.1 dB | LPIPS ≤ 0.05, ≥ 30 dB |
| 2 held-out portraits, same rows as the first run | 0.062, 25.7 dB (first run 0.083, 24.2) | 0.074, 25.8 dB (0.098, 24.9) | |
| training faces, unseen motions | 0.033 | | |
| mouth, jaw open, new faces | 0.044 | 0.054 | ≤ 0.08 |

- The gap between training and new faces shrank from about 3x to about 25% (about 40% on harder faces). The 256 px-core network sits on the LPIPS bar (0.0501 in the evaluation, 0.0485 in an independent rerun). Both miss PSNR and the self-reenactment criterion; the self-reenactment gap is the same on training faces, so more identities will not close it. The new-face score rests on 8 shared motions, so its margin is about one standard deviation.
- The portrait shortfall is not a portrait-versus-video domain effect: portraits that were in training score like the video faces. One held-out portrait is simply a hard identity. With two held-out portraits this cannot be separated further.
- Against the per-avatar baked Gaussian head on the same frames: the network is better on the open-jaw mouth for all three avatars checked; the bake is better on the whole frame for the hard identity (0.057 vs 0.072).
- Hybrid (offline test): rendering the face with the bake and pasting the 512 px network's output into a feathered region around the mouth is better than the bake alone on every metric for all three avatars, and gives the best whole-frame result on the hard identity (LPIPS 0.056 vs 0.057 for the bake and 0.072 for the network). Where the network already generalises, the network alone is as good or better. The network runs on a 256 px mouth crop at a quarter of its full-frame cost, but the browser would then need both the bake and the network's input data. No seam was measurable with the 512 px network.
- Browser run on the M4 (Chrome, fp16): the server streams 30 KB per frame (the 5,023 head vertices and the camera); the page splats, runs the 256 px-core network and draws on one GPU queue. 24.96 fps over 10 minutes, 0.31% of frames missed, frame latency median 23.1 ms (p90 26.6). In-page checks: splat 96-105 dB against the CUDA renderer, network output 57.5-58 dB against PyTorch. A mild drift late in the run (median 22.5 to 24.2 ms) did not lower the frame rate.

**Open conversation models instead of the OpenAI APIs: a cascaded open stack is the leading candidate for English on one A100; Japanese is undetermined; PersonaPlex is not adopted.**

- Stack: streaming ASR, Qwen3.5-4B served by vLLM, and Qwen3-TTS, orchestrated by the open-source speech-to-speech server, which speaks the same Realtime protocol as the app's existing backend.
- First reply has two modes, set by the end-of-turn detector rather than by compute:

| | turn judged complete | turn judged incomplete | share incomplete on these clips |
|---|---|---|---|
| English | 1.21 s | 2.42 s | 10/19 |
| Japanese | 1.27 s | 2.44 s | 3/19 |
| English, 300 ms hold after a complete turn | 0.72 s | 2.40 s | |
| PersonaPlex (English only) | 0.52 s median | | |

- The first turn of a session is slower (2.1 s English, 3.5 s Japanese). What the listener hears through the avatar adds about 0.8 s: measured on the same GPU as the avatar, about 2.0 s on complete turns and 3.2 s on incomplete ones, before the browser.
- The server's default 800 ms hold after a turn sets the complete-turn latency; PersonaPlex's 0.52 s is below that hold alone. Shortening the hold to 300 ms brings English to 0.6-0.8 s at a cost of one premature reply in 20 turns, in each language.
- Interruptions: the open stack detected, stopped and answered the interrupting question in English. PersonaPlex talked through 4-5 s of a 5.2 s interruption and never answered it, in all five trials, and spoke over the user on 3 of 20 turns.
- The open stack and the avatar fit together on one 40 GB A100 (36.3 GiB peak, about 2.8 GiB spare, GPU 94% busy while streaming), so one session per GPU.
- Speech recognition on clean synthetic test audio: English 19/20, Japanese 18-19/20. Japanese answer quality was probed with one ambiguous question (時間帯), which the model misread; that is not enough to judge Japanese.
- Two defects in the speech-to-speech server were found and fixed locally: a 1 s stall at the end of every reply, and Japanese replies not split into sentences.
- Not measured: OpenAI's own latency on the same clips, real (noisy) speech, more than one session.

**Multi-user capacity: about 2 photoreal sessions per A100 however they are hosted; motion-only sessions scale to 16 per A100 as separate processes.**

| per A100-80GB | one process | one process per session |
|---|---|---|
| motion only (rendering in the browser) | 8-9 | 16 (memory-bound) |
| motion + mesh render | 3 | 4 |
| motion + GAGAvatar 512 | 2 | 2 |

- Photoreal is bound by GPU render time (about 11 ms per frame); batching the render gains at most 5%. Batching the motion decode across sessions is 2x at 2 sessions and 6.5x at 16 in launches and CPU time per round, and would need a scheduler inside one process.
- In one process, sessions stop scaling at 12 while the GPU is only about a quarter busy, so the limit is the Python process itself. The app also left torch's CPU thread count at its default, which costs about two sessions per process; an opt-in cap is now in the app and on for the lab profile.
- These are A100 numbers; for the 24 GB L4 only the memory bounds carry over (at most one photoreal session per process).

**Session limit, per-user isolation and login: implemented, each behind an option that is off by default.** A configurable per-session time limit (counted from when media starts playing), per-visitor isolation of uploaded avatars, and an optional sign-in through Hugging Face accounts. The sign-in needs one more dependency and the Space's OAuth setting before it can be enabled; a real sign-in has not been tested yet.

**UniLS: integrates technically as a 4 s option, but brings no latency gain, and its licence rules out public use for now.**

- On our split UniLS scores LVE 8.92 / MHD 1.98 / FDD 22.2 against 8.55 / 1.91 / 27.0 for our 1 s model and 7.85 / 1.77 / 33.4 for the 4 s release (one sample per clip, so the ~0.3 mm gap is within noise). It runs at 27x realtime per 4 s chunk on an A100 and drives our mesh and GAGAvatar renderers.
- Its 4 s chunk is built into the released weights; a 1 s sliding window shows the same seam jumps as our sliding-window experiment. A 1 s retrain is estimated at 150-200 A100-hours on non-commercial data.
- Its style input is a raw 4 s motion clip per avatar, not a learned embedding.
- Feeding the other speaker's voice made the face calmer in one synthetic test, with no nodding; the listening behaviour is not established.
- The code has no licence and the weights and paired data are non-commercial (CC-BY-NC-4.0).

**FacePlex: not usable as a baseline yet.** No code, weights or data are released. It is also not a motion model that can be swapped in: it reads PersonaPlex's internal states and replaces the conversation model and the motion model together. Its quoted 122 ms is compute latency on an H200, excluding the decision to reply and rendering.

## 2. Other work since 2026-10-13

- The avatar now idles and blinks before the first reply with the 1 s model in the conversation modes. It used to hold its resting frame until the first reply, because the opening chunk of the 1 s model was shorter than the playback prebuffer.
- Two photoreal sessions in one server process no longer interfere. They shared one renderer state, so a second visitor choosing another face switched the first visitor's face; fixed and tested with two concurrent sessions.
- Streamlit was updated to 1.65.0, and the Space now opens on the 1 s model.
- Fallback demo recordings exist for live-demo failures: conversation clips with pauses on two avatars and a before/now comparison of blinks and idle motion.

## 3. Corrections to the 2026-10-13 notes

- "FacePlex has weaker listening than UniLS": the FacePlex paper reports better listening scores than UniLS (FDD 26.6 vs 30.2, user rating 3.30 vs 2.60), with four reaction types and no emotion control in either model.
- "UniLS can replace the baseline within one week": as released it brings back the 4 s latency floor, and its weights are non-commercial.
- "UniLS uses time-series style embeddings": the style input is a raw 4 s motion clip.

## 4. Open questions / proposed next directions

1. Browser renderer: the network now generalises much better but still misses PSNR and self-reenactment. Options: a stronger network for fast laptops, the per-avatar bake as the default, or the hybrid above as a mouth upgrade for the bake, chosen per avatar. Licensing is cleared for non-commercial use, and the browser design keeps FLAME on the server: the browser receives mesh vertices, never the FLAME model.
2. Conversation: test the open stack on real speech and against OpenAI's latency; tune the end-of-turn hold; probe Japanese answer quality with several questions.
3. Capacity: client-side rendering plus one process per session is the path to many users per GPU; a decode scheduler across sessions is the next step if more is needed.
4. UniLS as a lab-only 4 s option for a live comparison, if wanted; any public use needs a code licence from its author.
